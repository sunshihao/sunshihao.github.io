# Australia to introduce mandatory reporting after OpenAI hack to combat artificial intelligence ‘going rogue’

Government flags criminal law changes to ensure companies can’t argue in future ‘that someone didn’t know the computer was loaded’

Tess Ikonomou and Josh Butler

Tue 10 Nov 2026 01.00 EST

First published on Mon 9 Nov 2026 21.31 EST

OpenAI will not face criminal penalties over the Medicare data breach, but the prime minister has flagged changes to Australia’s criminal law to crack down on future breaches by artificial intelligence companies.

Anthony Albanese on Tuesday released the recommendations of a “rapid review” into the June breach of a Medicare statistics reporting service portal administered by Services Australia, but not the findings or the report itself, which he committed to releasing in the future.

A key recommendation is for the Attorney-General’s Department, the Australian federal police and home affairs to consider introducing “penalties for egregious regulatory non-compliance” and a “failure to prevent” offence, which would criminalise developing an AI model that engaged in harmful conduct while failing “to take reasonable steps to prevent that harm”.

The rapid review came in response to an AI agent, developed by OpenAI, accessing data from Services Australia and three other systems in June. The company did not notify the Australian government until mid-September despite becoming aware of the incident itself in August, prompting criticism from Labor and Albanese.

Guardian Australia revealed last month OpenAI used AI to help write the email to notify Australian authorities.

Albanese said the government would set up a new AI safety regulator and implement six key obligations on AI companies:

- Maintaining security controls across all stages of development and deployment.
- Identifying and managing serious risks associated with their models.
- Providing mandatory notification of incidents to the Australian government rapidly.
- Allowing independent evaluation of their models.
- Maintaining and publishing safety and security frameworks to ensure good governance, risk management and transparency.
- Responding to updated standards and rules as technologies change.

The review recommended the federal government “consider mandatory, time-bound AI safety reporting”, including around AI agents behaving in ways their creators may not have intended.
