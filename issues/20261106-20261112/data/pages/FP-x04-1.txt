Rep. Jim Jordan will lead a much-diminished House GOP as minority leader next year, beating out Majority Leader Steve Scalise in his third attempt to lead Republicans on Capitol Hill.

Jordan won the secret ballot vote 106-73, according to two people present for the tally.

A co-founder of the House Freedom Caucus and prominent MAGA figure, [Jordan pitched himself](https://www.politico.com/live-updates/2026/11/04/congress/jim-jordan-minority-leader-01148902) as best prepared to engage in political combat with the incoming Democratic House majority, noting he was “battle tested on the oversight front” in a letter to members. He currently chairs the House Judiciary Committee and previously chaired the Oversight Committee.

He assumes the top job after catastrophic losses for Republicans last week prompted Speaker Mike Johnson to stand down from leadership and sparked introspection in the GOP ranks about how to rebuild the party. Many viewed Jordan as a needed change of direction compared to Scalise, who has been in the top leadership ranks for more than a decade.

Jordan, 62, ran to be minority leader in 2018 but lost to California Rep. Kevin McCarthy, who went on to become speaker. After McCarthy was ousted from that job in 2023, Jordan tried and failed to replace him — losing a secret-ballot caucus vote to Scalise along the way.

A group of moderate Republicans kept Jordan from getting the speakership at the time, but many of those opponents will no longer serve in Congress.

Jordan had been laying the groundwork in the meanwhile to mount another run for leader, improving his fundraising and traveling widely to campaign with GOP colleagues. While Jordan needed a majority of the entire House to become speaker in 2023, he needed only a majority of the Republican conference to win the minority leader job.

It’s unclear what comes next for Scalise, who first entered the leadership ranks in 2014. He pitched himself as being best positioned to help Republicans win the majority in 2028 — something some Republicans are doubting is even possible after the blue wave last week.

## Senior House Republican **pushing for changes** to permitting bill

The chair of the Energy and Commerce Committee said there are “more details to work out” as senators try to pass bill.

By [Kelsey Brugger](https://www.politico.com/staff/kelsey-brugger) and [Nico Portuondo](https://www.politico.com/staff/nico-portuondo)

11/10/2026, 2:05pm ET

* Share:

House Energy and Commerce Chair Brett Guthrie said Tuesday he’s looking to make changes on “grid reliability” and other electricity provisions in the bipartisan permitting bill that’s currently working its way through the Senate.

In an interview with POLITICO, the Kentucky Republican said he was “encouraged” and “optimistic” while he continues to negotiate with the key Senate players.

“We’re continuing to work — there’s still more details to work out,” he said. “But I’ve been real pleased with the Senate listening to us … The Senate is not trying to pass a bill and say take it or leave it to the House, they are working with us.”

But Guthrie’s push for changes come as Senate Democrats are also seeking amendments to the bill before its final passage. While Senate negotiators have promised a “healthy amendment process,” it’s unclear exactly how many changes can be made without upsetting the delicate bipartisan balance of the bill.

Any major changes to the transmission section — a key component for Democrats to bring more clean energy on the grid — could upend the deal, Guthrie acknowledged. Historically, Republicans have been wary of socializing the cost of building massive power lines across red states to ship power to blue states. And if the House makes changes once it receives the measure, it would require the Senate to consider the package a second time, at which point coalitions could fall apart — if there’s even enough time to vote again before the end of the year.

“We know there are parameters that we can operate within and we are trying to operate within those,” he said. “The details are still being worked out.”

Nearly all Senate Republicans and more than 20 Democrats [voted Monday night to advance](https://subscriber.politicopro.com/article/2026/11/senate-votes-to-advance-bipartisan-permitting-reform-bill-01157283) the permitting bill that was hashed out by the top Democrats and Republicans on the energy and environment committees. Those four senators — Sheldon Whitehouse, Martin Heinrich, Shelley Moore Capito and Mike Lee — briefed the House Problem Solvers Caucus Tuesday.

Democrats Whitehouse and Heinrich have sold the measure as a way to lower electricity costs and buoy clean energy at a time when American voters made clear that affordability is a priority during last week’s blue tsunami election.

The measure is expected to pass the Senate — particularly after Monday night’s 73 to 25 successful procedural vote — but the House is more of an open question, even without Guthrie’s proposed tweaks. A small but vocal group of Republicans have in the past objected to any legislation that would cement approval for wind power projects — a position reinforced Monday, when President Donald Trump said [he wouldn’t approve wind permits](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/11/09/trump-says-he-will-not-approve-wind-permits-as-part-of-permitting-bill-01157629) despite this provision being a central part of the permitting package the White House brokered with senators.

Meanwhile, Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, who is expected to be speaker next year, has said the bill [needs more House input](https://www.politico.com/live-updates/2026/11/05/congress/jeffries-opposes-senate-permitting-deal-demands-more-house-input-01153083) amid continued opposition from some environmental organizations and tribal groups that have taken issue with the historic preservation provisions in the bill.

After the Problem Solvers Caucus meeting, Democratic Rep. Salud Carbajal of California underscored the difficulty some Democrats are wrestling with.

“In concept, it’s obviously a good bill, but it’s got a lot of challenges,” he said. “It’s got the tribal consultation issue, which is a great concern. It also does nothing to all the offshore wind projects that he already sabotaged and the buyouts that occurred.”

## Capitol agenda: GOP leaders face a **post-rout reckoning**

The House and Senate GOP are poised to hold separate elections Tuesday to decide who will serve as the top Republicans in the next Congress.

Republican leaders are fighting [to keep their power on Capitol Hill Tuesday](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/11/10/house-gop-elections-housecleaning-01157975) after stunning midterm losses last week.

The House and Senate GOP are poised to hold separate elections Tuesday to decide who will serve as the top Republicans in the next Congress. The winners are expected to immediately start charting a course back to governing relevance after Democrats gained the largest majorities won by any party in decades and voters delivered a firm rebuke of President Donald Trump.

How it’ll go: House Republicans will hold a candidate forum at 9 a.m. to hear from members running for all leadership positions, and a secret ballot will begin at 1:30 p.m.

Senate Republicans will meet at 9 a.m. It’s unclear how long their forum will continue — with some members pushing for a vote to delay the election to [give more time for deliberations](https://www.politico.com/newsletters/inside-congress/2026/11/09/jeffries-faces-big-choice-on-trump-01156178). But senior Republicans expect that push to fail and to know who will lead the conference by the end of the day.

What to expect: The House elections will pit existing Republican leaders vying to keep their top spots against insurgents who are hoping to capitalize on a desire from some for a clean slate.

Speaker Mike Johnson is bowing out, leaving Majority Leader Steve Scalise and Judiciary Chair Jim Jordan to battle it out for the No. 1 perch.

Jordan is pledging a fresh look for the House GOP’s top echelon and has swiped [at Johnson and Scalise](https://www.politico.com/live-updates/2026/11/04/congress/jim-jordan-minority-leader-01148902) for having too often “operated without a clear plan to survive the hour or the day in front of us.”

Scalise is seeking to fend off the throw-the-bums-out sentiment by drawing on his experience as a fundraiser and a set of personal relationships he has closely tended for years.

Thune appears to be on a path to reelection today as Senate GOP leader, despite calls from a handful of senators, like Sen. Ron Johnson [for a delay](https://www.politico.com/live-updates/2026/11/05/congress/senate-gop-leadership-delay-ask-01152558). One benchmark to watch: Sixteen Republicans [voted for a similar delay in 2022](https://www.politico.com/news/2022/11/16/senate-gop-reelects-mcconnell-as-leader-00067541), when Mitch McConnell sought his final term as leader.

Thune said Monday Republicans will be given plenty of space to talk through how they are feeling after the midterms. He’s pitching himself as ready to lead the conference amid an uncertain final two years of the Trump administration and a lot of 2028 jockeying.

“The job is not for the faint of heart, for sure,” Thune told POLITICO on Monday. “But I feel like I’ve got something to offer and to contribute ... as we think about now having to chart a slightly new direction.”
