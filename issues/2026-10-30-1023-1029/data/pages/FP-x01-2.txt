# What we know so far about deadly Spain train crash

Two trains crashed south of the Spanish capital Madrid on Wednesday morning, on the high-speed line to Seville.

At least 69 people were killed and dozens injured in the collision, when a state-operated Renfe high-speed train smashed into the back of a stationary train operated by the Iryo company near the town of Urda.

It is one of the worst rail disasters in Europe's modern history, and Spain's worst crash since 2013. Three days of national mourning have been declared in the country. 

Here is what we know about the crash so far.

## Where and when did the crash happen?

The crash happened at 08:53 (07:53 GMT) between municipalities Mora and Orgaz, and about 140km (100 miles) south of Madrid, according to an account given by the Spanish government.

The privately run Iryo train was stationary on the state-run Renfe train barrelled into the back of it at high speed. Iryo said its train was "struck from behind" by a Renfe train "due to causes that are still unknown".

The crash caused some carriages to fall down an embankment.

All train traffic on the line was stopped at stations along the route.

Renfe said services between Madrid, Ciudad Real and Andalusia were suspended.

A passenger on board one of the trains told local media there was a heavy impact about 40 minutes into the journey.

## What caused the crash?

Spanish Transport Minister Óscar Puente said a computer failure at about 08:30 on Wednesday morning led to the initial halt of six moving trains, including the two trains that subsequently collided.

"In the event of a system failure, the control centre takes over," he said, adding it was the control centre that decided to allow the trains to move again, without any speed restrictions. 

That decision, Puente said, was made "under inexplicable circumstances". It resulted in the Renfe train crashing into the Iryo train, which was stationary on the tracks about 25km (15 miles) ahead.

Audio recordings obtained by Spanish media between train controllers working in Madrid for a rail infrastructure company in Adif in Madrid and the train drivers appear to show that one high-speed train was given clearance to move at 08:36 even though another one ahead had stalled.

In one recording, the controller tells the driver of the Renfe train to disconnect and reconnect the LZB mechanism that monitors train speeds, and then resume the journey at normal speed.

"Disconnect and reconnect LZB. Continue your journey and then proceed under [Lateral Signalling Block], at normal speed with normal operation," the control centre operator says to the driver.

The driver replies, "At normal speed?" To which the controller says: "Yes."
