Rapid Recap
Anthropic is officially headed for the public market in what is expected to be a historic offering for a 5-year-old company that's been at the heart of the generative artificial intelligence boom. 
In a Form S-1 filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission, Anthropic said it plans to list on the Nasdaq under ticker symbol "ANT."
The company behind Claude confidentially filed with the SEC in June, giving the agency time to review its financials before they were made available to the public and prospective investors. 
Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei co-founded the company in 2021 after he and a small group of researchers defected from OpenAI, now its chief rival. Anthropic's Claude model and services have captivated the corporate world, as companies have opened their wallets in an effort to crack new growth opportunities and find internal efficiencies. 
Expansion has accelerated in 2026, driven largely by the popularity of AI coding tool Claude Code, and powerful models including Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5. The company has been racing to maintain an edge over competitors like OpenAI and Google, and faced sharp criticism over its positions on safety, regulation and national security, leading to two high-profile disputes with the U.S. government in recent months.
Anthropic's offering, if it doesn't get derailed, will land just a few months after Elon Musk's SpaceX debuted on the Nasdaq in by far the biggest IPO on record, ultimately raising $85.7 billion. It's been a volatile stretch for SpaceX shares, which finished last week slightly below where they opened in June.
As it prepares to hit the market, Anthropic has to contend with severe jitters on Wall Street as concerns grow over whether spending on AI services will justify the trillions of dollars in expected infrastructure costs. So-called neoclouds like CoreWeave and Nebius have been hammered of late, losing over 30% of their value in October as credit risk is rising across the data center space.

Amazon is still one of the AI lab's biggest backers

Anthropic stands out with 900% revenue retention rate

VIDEO5:3105:31
Anthropic's profitability is 'far ahead' of expectations: Analyst
Squawk Box Asia
When it comes to getting customers to pay more, Anthropic is currently in a class of its own. 
The company's net revenue retention, or NRR, stood at around 900% as of Sept. 30, according to the prospectus. That means existing customers are increasing their spending by ninefold from one year to the next. 
"Our customers' journey using Claude often begins with a single use case before expanding across departments, applications, and workflows," Anthropic said. "As our models become more capable, customers often deepen our partnerships — they find more ways to embed Claude into their workflows, increasing the scope of what Claude can accomplish within their enterprises."
For the median public cloud software company as of January, the number was 108%, according to data from Altimeter Capital partner Jamin Ball.
Anthropic said new models that it's released have lowered the costs of specific tasks, resulting in customers increasing their adoption.
"Anthropic net revenue retention is NINE HUNDRED PERCENT," Austin Allred, founder of Gauntlet AI, wrote on X. "What a ridiculous stat."

Mon, Oct 26 20266:53 PM EDT
Wild six weeks since Amodei called to slow down the pace of AI model development 
Six weeks ago Amodei rocked the tech sector by publishing an essay that urged AI companies to slow how quickly they improve their most advanced models. Now he's knocking on the door of the Nasdaq, after showing historic growth rates. 
Amodei proposed a three-step plan aimed at tempering the pace of development without "sacrificing commercial advantage or the United States' lead in AI."
The call for a slowdown followed stark warnings from industry researchers about AI's growing potential to cause catastrophic harm. Other AI leaders, including OpenAI's Altman and SpaceX's Musk, quickly expressed support for Amodei's proposal. 
Anthropic is actively working to implement the first step of the plan, which is to embed third-party evaluators within the company to help assess risks and verify safety practices. The company has inked agreements with organizations including Accenture, METR and SecureBio, and it's in discussions with others.

Anthropic is trying to get to market at a precarious moment for the AI trade.

Anthropic's strained relationship with the Trump administration
US President Donald Trump during an executive order signing in the Oval Office of the White House in Washington, DC, US, on Monday, Aug. 10, 2026.
Bonnie Cash | Bloomberg | Getty Images
Anthropic has had two major disputes with the Trump administration this year.
In June, the company had to temporarily disable access to two of its most advanced models, Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5, in order to comply with an export control directive from the government that cited "national security authorities." Anthropic restored the models after roughly two weeks of tense negotiations.
In March, Anthropic was banned by the Pentagon after discussions about how the military could use its AI models spiraled out of control. The company sued the Trump administration to try to reverse the blacklisting. In late August, a San Francisco federal judge ruled that the Pentagon's banning of Anthropic was illegal, and litigation in Washington, D.C., is ongoing.

Anthropic worries climate change can hurt its bottom line
Anthropic cautioned investors that climate change poses a risk to its operations and bottom line. 
"The physical effects of climate change may increase our operating costs over time, as higher average temperatures can increase the power consumption and water usage required to cool equipment at the facilities on which we depend," the filing states.
Computer processing demands for artificial intelligence are increasing air pollution from power plants, backup diesel generators and methane-gas burning turbines that supply electricity to data centers, according to research by Caltech and UC Riverside.
Anthropic is the largest single customer of SpaceX's AI division and has agreed to spend $3.1 billion per month on compute capacity from Elon Musk's defense tech conglomerate. 
SpaceX's Greater Memphis facilities, known as Colossus and Colossus 2, provide the compute capacity to Anthropic and are powered by methane-burning turbines. Those have made SpaceX the subject of multiple lawsuits over alleged Clean Air Act violations and noise pollution.

Canadian land exploration firm Anteros Metals had 'ANT' ticker before Anthropic
Anthropic has chosen "ANT" for its Nasdaq listing, but on the Canadian Securities Exchange, that same ticker is used by a six-person pre-revenue company called Anteros Metals, which does land prospecting and buys and sells mineral rights to larger developers. 
Anteros was founded in 2022, and went public to raise funding on the Canadian Securities Exchange in 2025, Chris Morrison, a director and the company's vice president of investor relations, told CNBC in an interview. Anteros is based in the province of Newfoundland and Labrador, on an island northeast of mainland Canada, with a cash balance of $394,673 in Canadian dollars as of June 30, according to a filing.
"ANT" was an obvious idea when the time came to pick a ticker symbol at Anteros. It is the first three letters of the company's name.
"We were a little bit reserved about using 'ANT' because we were thinking, you know, an ant, it's something very small, it's almost like a pest to a lot of people," Morrison said. "But our director and founder Bill [Kennedy], he kept saying ants can carry 10 times their weight." 
Anteros is an underdog, going up against more powerful rivals including New Found Gold Corp. and Vale Base Metals, Morrison said.
On Monday, Anteros found out about Anthropic's plans, along with everyone else. 
"We are all of a sudden very excited because we're thinking that, you know what, at the very least, a lot more people are going to are going to find out that we exist," said Morrison. Anteros did not hear from Anthropic, but he said he has personal and business subscriptions from Anthropic.
"At least in my household, we are big Claude fans," Morrison said, adding that his wife has been using Anthropic's artificial intelligence assistant too.
Then again, he said he and other board members also have OpenAI subscriptions.

How the tech industry's youth and safety issues impact Anthropic
This photograph shows a figurine in front of the logo of the AI assistant "Claude" built by the US artificial intelligence safety and research company Anthropic during a photo session in Paris on February 13, 2026. (Photo by Joel Saget / AFP via Getty Images)
Joel Saget | Afp | Getty Images
Anthropic is monitoring the various child-safety-related trials and litigation facing tech companies like Meta, Alphabet and OpenAI.
The AI lab's S-1 filing explains how companies developing foundation models have come under fire over allegations that their technologies "have been and may in the future be alleged to have played a role in instances of suicide, acts of violence toward others, or serious self-harm, which have resulted and may in the future result in significant public scrutiny, litigation, criminal investigations, regulatory attention, and reputational harm."
Although Anthropic's terms of service require users to be at least 18 years old, the company said in its filing that "minors have accessed in the past, and may in the future access" its platform, which poses potential child-safety issues.
The filing notes that lawmakers have won hefty settlements and injunctive relief against other tech companies in cases involving "analogous theories of product-design or algorithmic harm," without mentioning specific firms. Meta said in August that it would pay nearly $17 billion in a settlement with a coalition of state AGs who alleged the design of the company's apps like Facebook and Instagram contributed to various mental-health-related harms on children, which the social media giant underplayed to the public.
Anthropic's filing also noted that there's "at least one case" involving a state AG's "criminal investigation against another company offering similar services," but the AI lab didn't cite specific details. Florida AG James Uthmeier revealed in April a criminal investigation into OpenAI over the company's ChatGPT service and allegations about its role in a 2025 mass shooting at Florida State University.

Anthropic’s phased-approach to lock-ups
Anthropic employees and early investors will be able to sell their shares on the first day the AI firm begins trading, followed by several scheduled dates in which IPO lock-ups expire. This phased approach deviates from the traditional IPO process, in which insiders are unable to sell their shares for 180 days following a company's Wall Street debut. 
Under Anthropic's staggered lock-up schedule, workers and insiders will be able to sell an unspecified number of Class A shares as time passes after the debut. The lock-ups open 27, 48, 62, and 76 days following the IPO, the filing explained.
SpaceX also implemented a staggered lock-up schedule when it went public in June.

Anthropic highlights risks around securing and forecasting adequate compute capacity 
Anthropic warned that its ability to develop and train cutting-edge AI models depends on its ability to access "enormous and increasing quantities of specialized compute." 
"If we fail to secure sufficient compute, or if we fail to effectively direct the limited compute we have across our workloads to develop and deploy models with capabilities at least comparable to those of our competitors, we risk falling behind the technological frontier," the filing says.
Additionally, the company said that forecasts for future compute usage are "inherently uncertain," which could lead to capacity shortfalls or underutilized capacity. Anthropic said it has underestimated its compute requirements in the past, which impacted its product development and deployment plans.

Pentagon clash may cause Anthropic 'material revenue losses'
U.S. Secretary of War Pete Hegseth gives a State of the Force address at Marine Corps Base Quantico on Sept. 30, 2026 in Quantico, Virginia.
Alex Wong | Getty Images
In its IPO filing, Anthropic warned investors that ongoing issues with the Trump administration and U.S. Department of Defense could cause "material revenue losses or business disruptions." 
As CNBC previously reported, the department labeled Anthropic a "supply chain risk to national security," and in February, President Donald Trump also ordered the U.S. government to stop using the company's models. 
In March, Anthropic filed a complaint in a federal court in the Northern District of California challenging that label as illegal, and at the same time, filed a petition for review in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit.
The California federal court ruled in Anthropic's favor, finding that the Pentagon's actions were "retaliatory acts meant to punish" the company. The court also found the actions violated the company's First Amendment rights, and had violated the AI company's Fifth Amendment rights to due process, the company noted in its IPO filing.
But the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals ruled in favor of the DOD, saying the supply chain risk designation was allowed under the Federal Acquisition Supply Chain Security Act. 
Anthropic said in its filing that it is "evaluating its next steps" with respect to the D.C. appeals court decision. The company has asked the full D.C. Circuit to rehear its appeal.
Today, the U.S. filed a notice of appeal to try to invalidate the decision from the Northern California district court.

Anthropic uses AI chips from Nvidia, AMD, Amazon and Google 
Jensen Huang, chief executive officer of Nvidia Corp., shows Vera Rubin computer modules during the keynote address at the Nvidia GTC in Berlin, Germany, on Wednesday, Oct. 21, 2026. 
Alex Kraus | Bloomberg | Getty Images
Anthropic said its pool of computing power includes Nvidia's GPUs, AMD's GPUs, AWS Tranium, and Google TPUs.
Anthropic treats all AI chips as a "single fungible pool" and it has developed technology that routes its AI models to whatever chip is the most efficient at the time. 
It's a sign that Anthropic won't tie itself to any one chip provider. OpenAI, which mostly uses Nvidia compute, has made similar moves to diversify the AI chips it uses. 
"Our multi-chip compute approach provides us with substantially greater chip access than relying on one platform and enables us to opportunistically capture the price-performance benefits of each chip family and generation," Anthropic said in its filing. 
All four of Anthropic's primary chip designers have large stakes or options to buy stakes in Anthropic, highlighting the strategic ties between chipmakers or hyperscalers and the AI labs. 
Anthropic's filing left the amount of total money it has committed to spending on compute blank, but the frontier lab signaled it will spend heavily. 
Separately, Anthropic detailed long-term commitments with SpaceXAI, Nscale, and Lambda to secure access to Nvidia chips. The company also said it could buy more than $20 billion in AMD chips. It said it was committed to spending on Amazon's Trainium chips at $110 billion through 2036, and $114.5 billion on Google TPUs through 2033. 
"We anticipate making significant investments for the foreseeable future, including in compute and infrastructure, to ensure we have the technological resources to maintain and expand our position at the frontier of AI," Anthropic said in the filing.

Anthropic's implied gross margins have exploded, but cost of compute may change that
Anthropic reported its cost of revenue as $2.38 billion as of June 30, 2026, booking $16.29 billion in revenue during the same period and implying a gross margin of 85%. 
That's an explosion from the implied 12% gross margin the company generated during the same period last year, on just over $1 billion in cost of revenue and $1.16 billion in revenue booked. 
However, Anthropic's S-1 states that operating margin and adjusted operating margin are expected to "fluctuate over time, particularly in periods when substantial incremental compute is delivered to us and we incur the associated expenses."
The prospectus also reveals that "substantially all of cost of revenue" is associated with compute and infrastructure costs, adding that smaller personnel and overhead-related costs are also factored in.

Amazon's deepening ties to Anthropic
Amazon's largest AI data center has seven completed buildings, with 30 total buildings planned on 1,200 acres in New Carlisle, Indiana, shown here on October 8, 2025.
Erin Black
Amazon remains one of Anthropic's biggest backers and strategic partners. The company has poured $13 billion into Anthropic since 2023, with the potential to invest $20 billion more. 
According to the filing, Amazon has separately agreed to provide up to $15 billion in financing, which will become available to Anthropic in $5 billion tranches. Each draw is tied to Anthropic hitting certain compute capacity milestones. 
Amazon holds $6.2 billion in outstanding notes that could convert into as many as 1.84 billion shares. Amazon's stake is capped at 12% of the company, the filing states. 
Amazon holds nonvoting shares and it doesn't have a board or observer seat at the startup. 
The companies have continued to deepen their partnership, with Anthropic becoming a key customer of Amazon's cloud computing and chips businesses. Amazon last year opened an $11 billion AI data center campus dedicated to training and running models from Anthropic, called Rainier. 
In April, Anthropic said it committed to spend at least $110 billion with Amazon for cloud services through April 2036, "regardless of actual usage." 
The agreement is "non-cancelable," except for in the event of a "material breach" by Amazon, the filing says. If Anthropic's actual spend falls short, it's required to pay Amazon the difference as a true-up. 
The filing doesn't state how much Anthropic has spent on Amazon's cloud services and Trainium AI chips to date.

Anthropic's asset-light model in numbers
Numbers have finally been put to Anthropic's asset-light model. 
The company stated $1.19 billion in net property and equipment on June 30, 2026, up from $19 million at year-end 2024 and $146 million at year-end 2025. That $1.19 billion includes $432 million of net property and equipment associated with finance leases.
It's a sharp increase, but not as sharp as its off-balance-sheet obligations. The company's active operating leases sat at $117 million at the end of 2025, but that number has sharply risen to $1.079 billion. The company also added an additional $443 million in financing leases during that time period.
